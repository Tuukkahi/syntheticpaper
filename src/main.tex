\begin{abstract}
    We introduce a learned low-dimensional semi-Lagrangian advection operator for reduced Kalman filtering and smoothing.
    A neural network predicts a low-rank parametrization of the advection, and the induced state-transition operator propagates the filter.
    A one-step approximation bound links the expected reduced-basis approximation error to the spectral tail of the model error covariance.
    Experiments on partially observed satellite time series demonstrate the reduced-basis convergence, scalability, uncertainty behavior, and reconstruction gains from the learned state model.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Spatio-temporal reconstruction problems arise in applications where a dynamic field must be inferred from noisy and incomplete or indirect observations.
Examples include remote sensing satellite data, where measurements are often spatially and temporally irregular and corrupted by noise.
In such settings, it is not sufficient to produce static point estimates by interpolation but the constructed fields should also be temporally consistent.
Moreover, reliable uncertainty quantification is essential for downstream applications.
Although the exact dynamical model is typically unknown, the evolution is can still be approximated by assuming evolution is dominated by advection.
While we often do not have the exact the underlying dynamical model, the dynamics are frequently well-approximated by transport models where evolve primarily through advection.

Kalman filtering provides a principled method to solve linear Gaussian inverse problems where a dynamical model is available.
However, for high-dimensional state and observation spaces such as satellite data, the classical Kalman filter is computationally infeasible.
Reduced basis methods, such as the one introduced in~\cite{solonen} address this by limiting the posterior updates to a fixed low-dimensional subspace.
This approach has been successfully applied in large-scale problems dynamic tomography and general imaging problems.~\cite{xraykfdr,opticalflowkfdr}

Classical transport-dominated dynamic modelling relies on optical flow or variational formulations to estimate motion between consecutive frame~\cite{horn,lukaskanade,tvl1}, while more recently neural networks have been employed to learn transport dynamics from data.~\cite{flownet,pwcnet,raft}
These approaches are primarily designed to estimate dense motion fields and therefore do not naturally extend to filtering problems where the dynamical model must propagate information across the domain.

We introduce a learned dynamical model tailored to Kalman filtering problems.
Instead of predicting dense motion fields, we construct a neural network that produces a low-dimensional parametrization of the advection operator.
The motion field is constructed to lie in a prescribed function space, thereby allowing explicit control over model complexity and improving robustness to noise and model discrepancy.

In addition to the algorithmic formulation, we derive a one-step expected approximation bound for the reduced filter.
This bound relates the expected error to the spectral tail of the state model error prior covariance and thus provides guidance for selecting the reduced basis.
We show that the theoretical bound matches an empirical convergence rate in a lower-dimensional setting with real-world data.
\section{Materials and methods}
\subsection{Problem formulation}
We consider the problem of recovering a time-evolving spatial field $u(s, t), s \in \mathbb{R}^2$ from partial and noisy observations.
The field is observed at discrete time instances $t_k$ on a spatial grid, and we denote by $x_k \in \mathbb{R}^d$ the vectorized state containing values of $u$ at the grid points at time $t_k$ and by $y_k \in \mathbb{R}^m$ the corresponding observations.
We assume a linear observation model
\begin{equation}
    y_k = H_k x_k + v_k, \quad v_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, R_k),
\end{equation}
where $H_k$ extracts observed components of the state.


The state evolution is governed by a transport dominated dynamical model, which we approximate using an advection operator parametrized by a neural network that can be written as a linear state model
\begin{equation}
    x_{k} = M_{k} x_{k-1} + w_{k} \quad w_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, Q_k),
\end{equation}
where $M_k$ is constructed from the learned advection field.

The objective is to estimate the posterior distributions $\pi(x_k \mid y_{1:k})$ and $\pi(x_k \mid y_{1:T})$ efficiently in a high-dimensional setting.

\subsection{Advection model}
We model the deterministic part of the state evolution with the advection equation that is in continuous form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:adv}
    \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + F \cdot \nabla u = 0.
\end{equation}
Here, quantity $u: \mathbb{R}^2 \times [0, \infty)  \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$, is transported by the vector field $F: \mathbb{R}^2 \times [0, \infty) \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^2$.


For each grid point $s_i$ at the arrival time $t_k$, the semi-Lagrangian update computes the value at $s_i$ by tracing it back to a departure point $\tilde{s}_i$ at the previous time step $t_{k-1}$.
The new value is then interpolated from the previous field at the departure point.
That is,
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{s}_i = s_i - F_k(s_i),
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    u_k(s_i) = I_h [u_{k-1}](\tilde{s}_i),
\end{equation}
where $I_h$ is the spatial interpolation operator, and $F_k$ the discrete advection field describing transport over one time step interval expressed in grid coordinates.
This is the standard semi-Lagrangian form that follows the vector field backwards to departure points~\cite[Chap.~7]{durran}.
The semi-Lagrangian methods have seen wide adoption in atmospheric modelling because they are stable over large grid-scale displacements without the stability time-step restrictions imposed by explicit Eulerian schemes~\cite{diamantakis}.

For a fixed advection field $F_k$, the mapping $u_{k-1} \mapsto u_{k}$ is linear in $u_{k-1}$ and after vectorizing the field to a grid, we write
\begin{equation}
    x_k = M(F_k) x_{k-1},
\end{equation}
resulting in the state-transition operator $M_k := M(F_k)$ in the Kalman filter.

In the experiments we apply $M_k$ matrix-free using nearest-point interpolation to avoid unwanted smoothing and constant boundary value replication for out-of-domain departure points.

\subsection{Neural network model}\label{subsec:nnmodel}
The semi-Lagrangian update requires the advection field $F_k$ at each time step.
We learn the field using a short sequence of previous images.
Instead of predicting pixel-wise advection fields on the full grid, we predict a low-dimensional parametrization of $F_k$.

The choice is motivated by the filtering setting, large modelling errors, and high noise of the data.
Dense optical flow methods can be sensitive to non-advective behavior in the images.
However, the Kalman filter requires a stable state transition operator as opposed to a high-resolution optical flow method.
Therefore, we constrain the neural network's output to a finite-dimensional vector space.
This allows as to exactly control the complexity of the model by selecting a basis for the vector fields reflecting the expected solution structure.

In this work, we choose quadratic polynomial advection fields,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{P}_2(\mathbb{R}^2, \mathbb{R}^2) =
    \left\{
        \begin{bmatrix}
            a_1 + a_2x + a_3y + a_4xy + a_5x^2 + a_6y^2\\
            b_1 + b_2x + b_3y + b_4xy + b_5x^2 + b_6y^2
        \end{bmatrix} \,
        \middle| \,
        a_i, b_i \in \mathbb{R}
    \right\}.
\end{equation}

Rather than predicting directly the polynomial coefficients, we predict the values of $F_k$ at six interpolation nodes $z_i \in \mathbb{R}^2$, $i=1, \ldots, 6$.
The network predicts one scalar component of the advection field at the interpolation nodes.
To recover the two-dimensional advection, we evaluate the same scalar-component network on the original image sequence and on the same sequence rotated counterclockwise by $90^{\circ}$ in the spatial dimensions.
The scalar output from the rotated input is then used as the second component of the vector field.
In the implementation, the original and rotated sequences are stacked along the batch dimension, so both components are evaluated in a single forward pass.

For each input sequence, the network output is therefore the vector
\begin{equation}
    [f_k(z_1), \ldots, f_k(z_6)] \in \mathbb{R}^{6}.
\end{equation}
The scalar output from the rotated input is interpreted as the second component in the original coordinate system.
\begin{equation}
    [F_k(z_1), \ldots, F_k(z_6)] \in \mathbb{R}^{6 \times 2}.
\end{equation}

We choose the interpolation nodes such that the associated Vandermonde matrix
\begin{equation}
    V_2 = (p_j(z_i))_{i,j=1}^{6} \in \mathbb{R}^{6 \times 6},
\end{equation}
is well conditioned, where $p_j$ denote the scalar quadratic monomials.
Because of the low dimensionality of the problem, the placing of the interpolation nodes can be done geometrically by setting one node at the origin and five at equally spaced non-symmetric points on the unit circle.

Predicting the nodal values instead of the basis coefficients improves the numerical scaling of the target, because a small change in the nodal values correspond to controlled and small changes in the resulting advection field.

The parametrization is independent of the grid resolution.
The images can be resized to the required input resolution, while the predicted polynomial field can be evaluated for the original grid.


For predicting the scalar components, we use a 28-layer ResNet neural network~\cite{resnet}.
For each input sequence, the network produces the six nodal values and the full two-component field is obtained by rotating the input sequence as described above.

\subsection{Synthetic training data generation scheme}
Because real-world satellite datasets do not have ground-truth advection fields for supervised learning, we train the neural network with synthetic training data.
By simulating data with a wide range of spatial structures, noise levels, and flow patterns, we aim at training a model that generalizes beyond a specific data source.
Each synthetic sample is generated on demand during training, eliminating need for large dataset storage and providing a virtually unlimited supply of training data.

Each synthetic sample consists of an image sequence and a target advection field used as the training target.
The advection field is quadratic, while the image sequence is generated with additional time-dependent perturbations and observation-like artifacts.
Thereby the network is trained to recover the dominant low-dimensional transport from general image sequences in the presence of noise and modelling discrepancies.

\subsection{Prior-based dimension reduction Kalman filter}
We seek optimal state estimates and uncertainty quantification for the advective time series with partial and noisy observations.
To correct the neural network-predicted advection with the incomplete observations, we employ the Kalman filter.

We begin with the general linear Gaussian state space model
\begin{align}
    x_{k} &= M_{k} x_{k-1} + W_{k}, \qquad W_{k} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, Q_{k})\\
    y_{k} &= H_{k}x_{k} + V_{k}, \qquad V_{k} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, R_{k}),
\end{align}
where $M_{k} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is the state evolution operator, and $H_{k} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times d}$ the observation forward operator.

In our application, $M_k$ is the semi-Lagrangian advection operator constructed from the vector field predicted by the neural network.
The observations are direct but partial, so $H_k$ extracts the observed pixels from the state vector.

The Kalman filter recursively computes the Gaussian densities
\begin{align}
    \pi(x_k \mid y_{1:k-1}) &\propto \exp\!\left( - \tfrac{1}{2} \| x_k - x_{k \mid k-1} \|_{\Sigma_{k \mid k-1}}^2 \right), \label{eq:filterprior} \\
    \pi(x_k \mid y_{1:k}) &\propto \exp\!\left( - \tfrac{1}{2} \left( \| x_k - x_{k \mid k-1} \|_{\Sigma_{k \mid k-1}}^2 + \| y_k - H_k x_k\|_{R_k}^{2}\right) \right) \label{eq:filterposterior}
\end{align}
through the prediction and update steps.
Here we use the precision-weighted Euclidean norm arising from Gaussian densities
\[
    \|z\|_{A}^2 := z^\top A^{-1} z.
\]
For the derivation of the formulas, refer, e.g.\ to ~\cite{kaipiosomersalo}.

Because the state dimension $d$ equals the number of image pixels, direct manipulation of the covariance matrices is computationally infeasible.
We therefore apply the prior-based dimension reduction approach of~\cite{solonen} where the prior-based dimension reduction to static inverse problem is extended for the non-stationary Kalman filter case.

\paragraph{Reduced state parametrization:}
We introduce a fixed projection matrix $P_r \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$ with $r \ll d$ and parametrize the posterior state estimate as
We use Matérn fields as generic random scalar fields as initial conditions.
The Matérn covariance function between two points $u$ and $v$ is
\begin{equation}
    \Sigma_{\nu}(u,v)
    = \sigma^2 \frac{2^{1-\nu}}{\Gamma(\nu)}
    \left(\frac{\sqrt{2\nu}}{\ell} \lVert u - v \rVert\right)^{\nu}
    K_{\nu}
    \left(\frac{\sqrt{2\nu}}{\ell} \lVert u - v \rVert \right),
\end{equation}
where $\nu$, $\ell$, and $\sigma^2$ are the smoothness, correlation length, and the marginal variance parameters, respectively.
$K_{\nu}$ is the modified Bessel function of the second kind.
Equivalently, Matérn fields are characterized by the Matérn-Whittle SPDE~\cite{whittle63,lindgren}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:maternspde}
    (\kappa^2 - \Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}} X = W,
\end{equation}
where $W$ is Gaussian white noise on $\mathbb{R}^d$, and $X$ has Matérn covariance with parameters $\alpha := \nu + d/2$, and $\kappa := \sqrt{2 \nu}/\ell$.
On the two-dimensional regular grids used here, samples are generated spectrally by evaluating the inverse FFT of
\begin{equation}
    c_\sigma \left( \kappa^2 + \Vert \xi_m \Vert^2 \right)^{-\alpha/2} \widehat{W}(\xi_m),
\end{equation}
for realizations of discrete Fourier transformed white noise $\widehat{W}$, where $\xi_m$ are the discrete fourier frequencies, and $c_\sigma$ is set so that the generated field has a marginal variance of $\sigma^2$.

The base advection field is produced by sampling two-dimensional nodal values on the interpolation nodes which is then mapped to polynomial coefficients.
The image sequence is then produced by recursively applying the semi-Lagrangian update to the base advection perturbed by spatially correlated Matérn random fields to simulate noise and modelling discrepancy.
The supervised training target, however, remains the unperturbed base field.

After applying advection, the frames are cropped to the model's internal resolution, scaled to $[0,1]$, thresholded with a random threshold to create sparse zero-valued regions, and augmented with a small number of stationary localized Gaussian artifacts.
These transformations expose the network to general sparsity and non-advective behaviour commonly seen in satellite datasets.

The training loss is evaluated on the full-grid polynomial advection fields.
For predicted $\widehat{F}$ and target $F$ fields, we use
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}(\widehat{F}, F) = \operatorname{MSE}(\widehat{F}, F) + \frac{1}{2} \operatorname{mean} \left| \Vert \widehat{F} \Vert_2 - \Vert F \Vert_2 \right|,
\end{equation}
where the norms in the second term are pointwise Euclidean norms of the advection field and the mean is taken over the grid points.

\subsection{Prior-based dimension reduction Kalman filter}
At each time step the filtering problem is a linear Gaussian inverse problem with a dynamically propagated prior density.
The relevant conditional densities are the prediction prior, the filtering posterior, and the fixed-interval smoothing posterior.
Given observations up to time $k-1$, the prediction prior density is
\begin{align}
    \pi(x_k \mid y_{1:k-1}) &= \mathcal{N}(x_{k \mid k-1}, \Sigma_{k \mid k-1}) \\
    &\propto \exp\!\left( - \tfrac{1}{2} \| x_k - x_{k \mid k-1} \|_{\Sigma_{k \mid k-1}}^2 \right).\label{eq:filterprior}
\end{align}
Throughout this section,
\begin{equation}
    \|z\|_{A}^2 := z^\top A^{-1} z,
\end{equation}
denotes the covariance-weighted quadratic form.
We also use the conditioning notation
\begin{equation}
    x_{k \mid \ell} := \mathbb{E}[x_k \mid y_{1:\ell}],
    \qquad \Sigma_{k \mid \ell} := \operatorname{Cov}(x_k \mid y_{1:\ell}),
\end{equation}
where $\ell$ denotes the time index up to which observations are used for conditioning.
After assimilating the observation $y_k$ under the likelihood $\pi(y_k \mid x_k) \propto \exp(- \tfrac{1}{2} \| y_k - H_k x_k\|_{R_k}^{2})$, the filtering posterior is
\begin{align}
    \pi(x_k \mid y_{1:k}) &= \mathcal{N}(x_{k \mid k}, \Sigma_{k \mid k}) \\
                          &\propto \exp\!\left( - \tfrac{1}{2} \left( \| x_k - x_{k \mid k-1} \|_{\Sigma_{k \mid k-1}}^2 + \| y_k - H_k x_k\|_{R_k}^{2}\right) \right).\label{eq:filterposterior}
\end{align}
Finally, the fixed-interval smoothing posterior is
\begin{equation}
    \pi(x_k \mid y_{1:T}) = \mathcal{N}(x_{k \mid T}, \Sigma_{k \mid T}).
\end{equation}
For the full Kalman prediction and update step formulas and the fixed-interval Rauch-Tung-Striebel smoother, see, e.g.,~\cite{kaipiosomersalo,sarkka}.
A direct application of the Kalman filter is infeasible even for moderately sized images because the state dimension $d$ equals the number of image pixels and its covariance is a dense $d \times d$ matrix.
Therefore, we apply the prior-based dimension reduction approach of~\cite{solonen}, which extends prior-based dimension reduction for static inverse problems to the non-stationary Kalman filter case.
The key idea is to represent the filtering covariance in a fixed low-dimensional subspace while keeping the state mean in the full state space.

\paragraph{Reduced state parametrization:}
Let $P_r \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$ be a fixed reduced basis matrix with $r \ll d$.
We restrict the filter update increment to $\mathcal{R}(P_r)$ by writing
\begin{equation}
    x_{k} = x_{k \mid k-1} + P_r \alpha_{k}, \quad \alpha_{k} \in \mathbb{R}^r.\label{eq:posteriorparametrization}
\end{equation}
Substituting~\eqref{eq:posteriorparametrization} into~\eqref{eq:filterposterior} gives the reduced posterior density
\begin{equation}
    \pi(\alpha_{k} \mid y_{1:k})
    \propto \exp\!\left(- \tfrac{1}{2} \left(
    \| P_r \alpha_{k} \|_{\Sigma_{k \mid k-1}}^2
    + \| y_k - H_k (x_{k \mid k-1} + P_r \alpha_{k}) \|_{R_k}^{2}
    \right) \right). \label{eq:reducedfilterposterior}
\end{equation}
We denote the mean and covariance of the reduced coordinate random variable $\alpha_k$ by
\begin{equation}
    \alpha_{k \mid k} := \mathbb{E}[\alpha_k \mid y_{1:k}], \qquad
    \Psi_{k|k} := \operatorname{Cov}(\alpha_k \mid y_{1:k}).
\end{equation}
The corresponding full-state posterior mean approximation is
\begin{equation}
    x_{k \mid k}^{(r)} = x_{k \mid k-1} + P_r \alpha_{k \mid k}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Prediction step:}
The prior state mean and covariance representation propagate using $M_k$ as
\begin{align}
    x_{k \mid k-1} &= M_{k} x_{k-1 \mid k-1},\\
    \Sigma_{k \mid k-1} &= (M_{k} P_r) \Psi_{k-1 \mid k-1} (M_{k} P_r)^{\top} + Q_{k},\label{eq:predcov}
\end{align}

\paragraph{Update step:}
The Gaussian~\eqref{eq:reducedfilterposterior} has posterior mean and reduced covariance
\begin{align}\label{eq:updatestep}
    \alpha_{k \mid k} &= \Psi_{k \mid k} (H_k P_r)^{\top}
                         R_{k}^{-1} (y_k - H_k x_{k \mid k-1}),\\
    \Psi_{k \mid k}   &= \left(
                            (H_k P_r)^{\top} R_{k}^{-1} (H_k P_r)
                            + P_{r}^{\top} \Sigma_{k \mid k-1}^{-1} P_r
                         \right)^{-1}\label{eq:psiupdate}
\end{align}
To evaluate the term $P_{r}^{\top} \Sigma_{k \mid k-1}^{-1} P_r$ efficiently, we apply the Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury identity.
Let $A_k$ be a square root of $\Psi_{k-1 \mid k-1}$, for example its Cholesky factor, so that $\Psi_{k-1 \mid k-1} = A_{k} A_{k}^{\top}$, and define
\begin{equation}
    B_k := M_k P_r A_k.
\end{equation}
Then from~\eqref{eq:predcov},
\begin{equation}
    \Sigma_{k \mid k-1}^{-1} = (B_k B_k^{\top} + Q_k)^{-1},
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    \Sigma_{k \mid k-1}^{-1} = Q_{k}^{-1}
    - Q_{k}^{-1} B_k
    (I + B_{k}^{\top} Q_{k}^{-1} B_k)^{-1}
    B_{k}^{\top} Q_{k}^{-1}.
\end{equation}
Thus, the inverse action of $\Sigma_{k \mid k-1}$ can be evaluated by applying $Q_{k}^{-1}$ and solving an $r \times r$ system.
When the precision actions of $R_k$ and $Q_k$ are available, the explicit inversions required are reduced from $d \times d$ to $r \times r$ systems.

In the implementation, we return the reduced posterior precision in Cholesky form
\begin{equation}
    \Psi_{k \mid k}^{-1} = L_k L_{k}^{\top},
\end{equation}
due to numerical stability and the subsequent RTS smoother recursion.

\paragraph{Recovery of pointwise uncertainty.}
$\Psi_{k \mid k}$ is the covariance of the reduced coordinate random variable $\alpha_k$.
The corresponding covariance contribution in the state space is
\begin{equation}
    \Sigma_{k \mid k}^{(r)} = P_r \Psi_{k \mid k} P_{r}^{\top}.
\end{equation}
Although this $d \times d$ matrix may be infeasible to form, its diagonal can be recovered as
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{diag}(\Sigma_{k \mid k}^{(r)})
    = \sum_{j=1}^{r} (P_r \Psi_{k \mid k}^{1/2})_{:, j}^{2},
\end{equation}
where the square is taken componentwise.
In the implementation, the diagonal is computed by triangular solves with the precision Cholesky factor, avoiding explicit inversion of $\Psi_{k \mid k}$ and formation of $\Sigma_{k \mid k}^{(r)}$.
This gives an efficient way to compute approximate pointwise variance fields in the original state dimension.

\paragraph{Choice of the reduced subspace.}
The reduced subspace basis $P_r$ controls both the accuracy and computational cost of the filter.
If $r=d$ and $P_r=I$, the above equations reduce to the standard full Kalman filter.
A natural choice would be to update $P_r$ from the leading eigenvectors of the current prior covariance.
In the filtering setting, however, this may suppress directions that become important later, as discussed in~\cite{solonen}.

In earlier applications of the prior-based reduced Kalman formulation, the reduced subspace basis has been constructed for example from leading singular vectors of an a priori chosen covariance matrix~\cite{xraykfdr} or from spline basis functions~\cite{opticalflowkfdr}.
Here, we choose $P_r$ from the leading eigenvectors of the model error covariance.
This basis choice connects the reduced filter error directly to the spectral tail of $Q_k$ in the one-step approximation bound below.
For the Matérn covariance model we use in our experiments, this tail has an explicit algebraic decay rate.
The basis vectors are scaled to unit norm to improve numerical conditioning.

\paragraph{Expected approximation error.}
For $A \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, let
\begin{equation}
    \Vert A \Vert_{*} := \operatorname{tr}\left( (A^{\top} A)^{1/2} \right)
\end{equation}
denote the trace norm.
\begin{proposition}[One-step dimension reduction update expected error]\label{prop:onestep}
    Suppose that $Q_k \succ 0$ and let the orthonormal columns of $P_r$ span its $r$ leading eigenvectors.
    Denote the eigenvalues of $Q_k$ by $q_{k,1} \geq \cdots \geq q_{k,d}$, and define
    \begin{equation}
        C_k := \kappa(\Sigma_{k \mid k}^{-1}), \qquad
        \beta_k := \lambda_{\max}(Q_{k}^{-1/2} \Sigma_{k\mid k-1} Q_{k}^{-1/2}).
    \end{equation}
    Then the filter posterior mean and covariance satisfy
    \begin{equation}
        \mathbb{E} \Vert x_{k \mid k} - x_{k \mid k}^{(r)} \Vert_{2}^{2} \leq C_k \beta_k \sum_{j > r} q_{k,j}, \qquad
        \Vert \Sigma_{k \mid k} - \Sigma_{k \mid k}^{(r)} \Vert_{*} \leq  C_k \beta_k \sum_{j > r} q_{k,j}.
    \end{equation}
    Moreover, for a two-dimensional Matérn covariance with smoothness $\nu$, there is a constant $\gamma_k > 0$, independent of $r$, such that
    \begin{equation}
        \sum_{j > r} q_{k,j} \leq \gamma_k r^{-\nu}.
    \end{equation}
    Consequently, both errors are bounded by $C_k \beta_k \gamma_k r^{-\nu}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Fix the prediction state $x_{k \mid k-1} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and covariance $\Sigma_{k \mid k-1}  \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$.
Let $\Pi_r$ denote the Euclidean orthogonal projector onto $\mathcal{R}(P_r)$.

Define the analysis increments
\begin{equation}
    u_k := x_{k \mid k} - x_{k \mid k-1}, \qquad
    u_k^{(r)} := x_{k \mid k}^{(r)} - x_{k \mid k-1}.
\end{equation}
In the increment variable, the full and reduced Kalman updates minimize
\begin{equation}
    \Phi_k(u)
    = \frac{1}{2} \Vert y_k - H_k(x_{k \mid k-1} + u) \Vert_{R_{k}}^{2}
    + \frac{1}{2} \Vert u \Vert_{\Sigma_{k \mid k-1}}^{2}.
\end{equation}
For the full filter, $\Phi_k$ is the quadratic objective associated with
the filtering posterior~\eqref{eq:filterposterior}.
For the reduced increments $u = P_r \alpha$, the same objective gives the corresponding
reduced quadratic in~\eqref{eq:reducedfilterposterior}.
Therefore, $u_k$ and $u_{k}^{(r)}$ are the minimizers of $\Phi_k$ over
$\mathbb{R}^d$ and $\mathcal{R}(P_r)$, respectively.

Define
\begin{equation}
    b_k := H_k^\top R_k^{-1}(y_k - H_k x_{k\mid k-1}).
\end{equation}
The full Kalman posterior precision is
\begin{equation}
\Sigma_{k \mid k}^{-1} = H_{k}^{\top} R_{k}^{-1} H_k + \Sigma_{k \mid k-1}^{-1}.
\end{equation}
Expanding $\Phi_k(u)$ then gives
\begin{equation}
    \Phi_k(u)=\frac{1}{2} u^\top \Sigma_{k\mid k}^{-1} u - b_k^\top u + \mathrm{const.}
\end{equation}
Because $\Sigma_{k\mid k}^{-1}$ is positive definite, this quadratic is strictly convex.
Its first-order conditions over $\mathbb{R}^d$ and $\mathcal{R}(P_r)$ give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:projector}
    u_k = \Sigma_{k\mid k} b_k, \qquad u_{k}^{(r)} = \mathcal{P}_{k,r} u_k,
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{P}_{k,r} := P_r
    (P_{r}^{\top} \Sigma_{k\mid k}^{-1} P_r)^{-1}
    P_{r}^{\top} \Sigma_{k\mid k}^{-1}
\end{equation}
is the $\Sigma_{k\mid k}^{-1}$-orthogonal projector onto $\mathcal{R}(P_r)$.
Then for any $v \in \mathbb{R}^d$,
\begin{equation}
    \Vert v - \mathcal{P}_{k,r}v \Vert_{\Sigma_{k \mid k}}^{2}
    \leq \Vert v - \Pi_r v \Vert_{\Sigma_{k \mid k}}^{2},
\end{equation}
and by the Rayleigh quotient bounds,
\begin{equation}
    \Vert v - \mathcal{P}_{k,r}v \Vert_{2}^{2}
    \leq C_k \Vert v - \Pi_r v \Vert_{2}^{2}.
\end{equation}
Moreover, for any centered random vector $V$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:expbound}
    \mathbb{E}\Vert V - \mathcal{P}_{k,r}V \Vert_{2}^{2}
    \leq C_k \operatorname{tr}((I - \Pi_r)\operatorname{Cov}(V)).
\end{equation}
We now apply~\eqref{eq:expbound} twice to get estimates for the mean and covariance.

Conditionally on $(y_{1:k-1})$, the filter innovation has zero mean, and hence so does $u_k$.
Together with~\eqref{eq:projector}, we get
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E} \Vert u_k - \mathcal{P}_{k,r}(u_k) \Vert_{2}^{2}
    = \mathbb{E} \Vert x_{k \mid k} - x_{k \mid k}^{(r)} \Vert_{2}^{2}
    \leq C_k \operatorname{tr}((I - \Pi_r) \operatorname{Cov}(u_k))
\end{equation}
For the Kalman update, the analysis increment covariance satisfies, in the Loewner order,
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Cov}(u_k) = \Sigma_{k \mid k-1}-\Sigma_{k \mid k} \preceq \Sigma_{k \mid k-1}.
\end{equation}
Thus,
\begin{equation}
    C_k \operatorname{tr}((I - \Pi_r) \operatorname{Cov}(u_k))
    \leq C_k \operatorname{tr}((I - \Pi_r) \Sigma_{k \mid k-1}).\label{eq:trbound1}
\end{equation}

For the covariance, let $V_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma_{k\mid k})$.
Observe that
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Cov}(V_k - \mathcal{P}_{k,r}V_k)
    = (I - \mathcal{P}_{k,r}) \Sigma_{k\mid k} (I - \mathcal{P}_{k,r})^{\top}
    = \Sigma_{k\mid k} - \Sigma_{k\mid k}^{(r)} \succeq 0,
\end{equation}
where the second equality follows by direct expansion using definitions of $\mathcal{P}_{k,r}$ and $\Sigma_{k\mid k}^{(r)}$.
So by applying~\eqref{eq:expbound},
\begin{align}
    \Vert \Sigma_{k\mid k} - \Sigma_{k\mid k}^{(r)} \Vert_{*} &= \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{k\mid k} - \Sigma_{k\mid k}^{(r)})\\
                                                              &= \mathbb{E}\Vert V_k - \mathcal{P}_{k,r}V_k \Vert_{2}^{2}\\
                                                              &\leq C_k \operatorname{tr}((I - \Pi_r)\Sigma_{k\mid k})
                                                              \leq  C_k \operatorname{tr}((I - \Pi_r)\Sigma_{k\mid k-1}), \label{eq:trbound2}
\end{align}
we obtain the same bound.

By the definition of $\beta_k$, $\Sigma_{k\mid k-1} \preceq \beta_k Q_k$, and thus
\begin{equation}
    C_k \operatorname{tr}((I - \Pi_r)\Sigma_{k\mid k-1})
    \leq C_k \beta_k \operatorname{tr}((I - \Pi_r) Q_k)
    = C_k \beta_k \sum_{j>r} q_{k,j}.
\end{equation}
Combining this spectral tail with~\eqref{eq:trbound1} and~\eqref{eq:trbound2} proves both one-step bounds.

By Weyl's asymptotic law~\cite[Chap. I, Sec. 3]{chavel} and the spectral representation
of the Matérn-Whittle SPDE~\eqref{eq:maternspde}, the ordered Matérn covariance  eigenvalues in two spatial dimensions satisfy
\begin{equation}
    q_{k,j} \asymp j^{-(\nu+1)}.
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
    C_k \beta_k \sum_{j>r} q_{k,j} \leq  C_k \beta_k \gamma_k r^{-\nu}.
\end{equation}
\end{proof}

The bound shows that the reduced basis approximation error inherits the spectral tail of the model error covariance.
For the two-dimensional Matérn covariance used here, this gives the expected squared error bound of order $r^{-\nu}$.
The constants $C_k$ and $\beta_k$ depend on the conditioning of the filtering posterior and the relation between the prediction covariance and model error covariance, so the bound is intended as convergence rather than a sharp practical error estimate.
\paragraph{Error propagation}
Denote the mean and covariance approximation errors at time $k$ by
\begin{equation}
    e_k := x_{k \mid k} - x_{k \mid k}^{(r)}, \qquad E_k := \Sigma_{k\mid k} - \Sigma_{k\mid k}^{(r)}.
\end{equation}
We assume a common initializatin for both the full and reduced filters, thus $e_0=0$ and $E_0 = 0$.
Then denote by $\tilde{\Sigma}_{k\mid k}$ the full Kalman covariance update at time $k$ from a reduced prior $\Sigma_{k\mid k-1}^{(r)}$, so that the local covariance error introduced from the reduced update at time $k$ is
\begin{equation}
    D_{k} := \widetilde{\Sigma}_{k\mid k} - \Sigma_{k\mid k}^{(r)} \succeq 0.
\end{equation}
In the same fashion we denote the gains associated with different covariances by
\begin{equation}
    K_k := \Sigma_{k\mid k} H_{k}^{\top} R_{k}^{-1}, \qquad
    \widetilde{K}_k := \widetilde{\Sigma}_{k\mid k} H_{k}^{\top} R_{k}^{-1}, \qquad
    K_{k}^{(r)} := \Sigma_{k\mid k}^{(r)}  H_{k}^{\top} R_{k}^{-1}.
\end{equation}

Now we may introduce recursion formulas for the filter errors.
\begin{lemma}[Filter error recursions]\label{lem:recursions}
    The mean and covariance errors at time $k$ satisfy
    \begin{align}
        e_k &= (I-K_{k}^{(r)} H_k)M_k e_{k-1} + E_k H_{k}^{\top} R_{k}^{-1} (y-H_k x_{k \mid k-1}),\\
        E_k &= (I-K_k H_k)M_k E_{k-1} M_{k}^{\top}(I-\widetilde{K}_k H_k)^{\top} + D_{k}.
    \end{align}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
We obtain the mean by direct substitution
\begin{align}
    e_k &= x_{k \mid k} - x_{k \mid k}^{(r)}\\
        &= M_k x_{k-1 \mid k-1} + K_k (y-H_k x_{k \mid k-1}) - K_{k}^{(r)} (y - H_k x_{k \mid k-1}^{(r)})\\
        &= (I- K_{k}^{(r)} H_k)M_k e_{k-1} + E_k H_{k}^{\top} R_{k}^{-1} y_k - E_k(H_{k}^{\top} R_{k}^{-1} H_{k} x_{k \mid k-1})\\
        &= (I-K_{k}^{(r)} H_k)M_k e_{k-1} + E_k H_{k}^{\top} R_{k}^{-1} (y-H_k x_{k \mid k-1}).
\end{align}
For the covariance, we observe first that for the prior error
\begin{equation}
    \Sigma_{k\mid k-1} - \Sigma_{k\mid k-1}^{(r)} = M_k E_{k-1} M_{k}^{\top},
\end{equation}
then again by direct substitution
\begin{align}
    E_k &= \Sigma_{k\mid k} - \widetilde{\Sigma}_{k\mid k} + D_{k}\\
        &= (I - K_k H_k) \Sigma_{k\mid k-1} - (I - \widetilde{K}_k H_k) \Sigma_{k\mid k-1}^{(r)} + D_k \\
        &= (I - K_k H_k) M_k E_{k-1} M_{k}^{\top} (I-\widetilde{K}_k H_k)^{\top} + D_k.
\end{align}
\end{proof}
For iterating the error recursions, for $0 \leq j<k$, define
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{F}_{k:j} &:= ((I-K_{k} H_{k})M_k) \cdots ((I-K_{j} H_{j})M_j),\\
    \widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{k:j} &:= ((I-\widetilde K_{k} H_{k})M_k) \cdots ((I- \widetilde K_{j} H_{j})M_j),\\
    \mathcal{F}_{k:j}^{(r)} &:= ((I-K_{k}^{(r)} H_{k})M_k) \cdots ((I-K_{j}^{(r)} H_{j})M_j). \label{eq:errorpropagators}
\end{align}
These error propagators allow us now to bound the approximation error for finite-time filter runs.
\begin{theorem}[Forward propagation of the errors]\label{thm:fixed-time-error}
    Fix $T < \infty$.
    Then for every $0 \leq k \leq T$,
    \begin{align}
        \mathbb{E} \Vert e_k \Vert_{2}^{2} \leq &
        \sum_{j=1}^{k} \Vert \mathcal{F}_{k:j}^{(r)} \Vert_{2}^{2}
        \Vert E_j \Vert_{*}^{2}
        \mathbb{E} \Vert b_k \Vert_{2}^{2}\\
        \Vert E_k \Vert_* \leq &
        \sum_{j=1}^{k} \Vert \mathcal{F}_{k:j} \Vert_2
        \Vert D_j \Vert_*.
        \Vert \widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{k:j} \Vert_2
    \end{align}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    Unrolling the recursions of Lemma~\ref{lem:recursions} with the error propagation operators~\eqref{eq:errorpropagators} and
    applying the Hölder inequality for Schatten norms yields
    \begin{align}
        \mathbb{E} \Vert e_k \Vert_{2}^{2}
        &= \sum_{j=1}^{k} \mathbb{E} \left\lVert \mathcal{F}_{k:j}^{(r)} E_j b_j \right\rVert_{2}^{2}\\
        &\leq \sum_{j=1}^{k}
        \left\lVert \mathcal{F}_{k:j}^{(r)} E_j \right\rVert_{2}^{2}
        \mathbb{E} \left\lVert b_j \right\rVert_{2}^{2}
        \leq \sum_{j=1}^{k} \left\lVert \mathcal{F}_{k:j}^{(r)} \right\rVert_{2}^{2}
        \Vert E_j \Vert_{*}^{2}
        \mathbb{E} \Vert b_k \Vert_{2}^{2},
    \end{align}
    and
    \begin{align}
        \Vert E_k \Vert_*
        &= \sum_{j=1}^{k} \left\lVert \mathcal{F}_{k:j} D_j \widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{k:j} \right\rVert_{*}\\
        &\leq \sum_{j=1}^{k} \left\lVert \mathcal{F}_{k:j} \right\rVert_2
        \Vert D_j \Vert_*.
        \left\lVert \widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{k:j} \right\rVert_2.
    \end{align}
\end{proof}
Suppose now that the eigenvectors of $Q_j$ remain unchanged throughtout the filtering interval,
like, e.g., in the Matérn case, and that $P_r$ spans its leading eigenvectors.
Then by choosing the worst approximated time index
\begin{equation}
    m_r = \operatorname{arg\, max}_{1 \leq j \leq T}
    \operatorname{tr}\left( (I-\Pi_r) Q_j \right),
\end{equation}
we would obtain the bounds of Proposition~\ref{prop:onestep} for the maximal errors
\begin{equation}
    \max_{1 \leq k \leq T} \mathbb{E} \Vert e_k \leq C_T \sum_{j > r} q_{m_r,j}
    \qquad \max_{1 \leq k \leq T} \Vert E_k \Vert_* \leq C_T \sum_{j > r} q_{m_r,j},
\end{equation}
for $C_T < \infty$, independent of $r$, and thus also the same Matérn rates.
The bound still depends on the filtering end time $T$.
For the time-uniform result, we will require further assumptions.

The first condition is a time-independent bound
\begin{equation}
    c_b := \sup_{j \geq  1} \mathbb{E} \Vert b_k \Vert_{2}^{2} < \infty.\label{eq:bbound}
\end{equation}
To discuss what that could mean, we can estimate it with full filter quantities.
First, denote the full filter innovation covariance by
\begin{equation}
    S_j := H_j \Sigma_{j \vert j-1} H_{j}^{\top} + R_j.
\end{equation}
Then following the arguments of the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:onestep},
\begin{align}
    \mathbb{E} \Vert b_k \Vert_{2}^{2}
    &= \operatorname(tr) \left( H_{j}^{\top} R_{j}^{-1} S_j R_{j}^{-1} H_{j} \right)\\
    &\leq \left\lVert \Sigma_{j \vert j}^{-1} \right\rVert_{2}^{2} \operatorname{tr}\left(\Sigma_{j \vert j-1}\right).
\end{align}
These quantities would translate into the maximum amplification by the posterior precision and the total prior variance.

In addition, we require exponential stability of the error propagators.
The parametrization of the posterior correction~\eqref{eq:posteriorparametrization} means that the complement of the reduced subspace is not corrected by the filter:
\begin{equation}
    (I-\Pi_r)(x_{k \mid k-1} + P_r \alpha_k) = (I-\Pi_r)(x_{k \mid k-1}).
\end{equation}
Therefore, the state model would need to contract the components of the state outside of $\mathcal{R}(P_r)$.

\subsection{Dimension reduction RTS smoother}
The fixed-interval Rauch-Tung-Striebel (RTS) smoother gives the algorithm to compute the state densities conditioned by both past and future observations
\begin{equation}
    \pi(x_{k} \mid y_{1:T}).
\end{equation}
For the derivation of the standard backward recursion RTS equations, we refer to any standard textbook, e.g.~\cite{sarkka}.

We express the smoothed state in the same reduced parametrization
\begin{equation}
    x_{k \mid T} = x_{k \mid k} + P_r \alpha_{k \mid T}.
\end{equation}

Using the stored Cholesky factors $\Psi_{k}^{-1} = L_k L_{k}^{\top}$ and state evolution operators $M_k$ from the forward pass, we define the whitened reduced state transition
\begin{equation}
    C_{k} := M_k P_r L_{k}^{-\top}.
\end{equation}

The reduced smoother gain becomes
\begin{equation}
    K_{k}^{s} = L_{k}^{\top}C_{k}^{\top} Q_{k}^{-1} - L_{k}^{\top} C_{k}^{\top} Q_{k}^{-1} C_{k} (I + C_{k}^{\top} Q_{k}^{-1} C_{k})^{-1} C_{k}^{\top} Q_{k}^{-1} \, \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d},
\end{equation}
where we again need to do matrix inversion in $r \times r$ dimensions.

The backward recursion is then
\begin{align}
    \alpha_{k \mid T} &= K_{k}^{s}(x_{k+1 \mid T} - M_k x_{k \mid k})\\
    \Psi_{k \mid T} &= \Psi_{k \mid k} + (K_{k}^{s} P_r) (\Psi_{k+1 \mid T} - \Psi_{k+1 \mid k}) (K_{k}^{s} P_r)^{\top}.
\end{align}
Similarly to the forward recursion, the standard RTS smoother is recovered with $P_r=I_{d \times d}$

\begin{algorithm}
    \caption{Reduced Kalman filter with neural network advection and optional RTS smoothing}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \Require Initial states $x_{-2},x_{-1},x_0$, projection $P_r$, initial $\Psi_{0|0}$, noise covariances $Q_k,R_k$, observations $y_{1:T}$
        \State Initialize $x_{0|0}$ and factorize $\Psi_{0|0}^{-1} = L_0 L_0^\top$
        \For{$k=1$ to $T$}
        \State $M_k \gets NN(x_{k-3:k-1|k-1})$
        \State Predict $x_{k|k-1} \gets M_k x_{k-1|k-1}$
        \State Form $\Sigma_{k|k-1} = (M_k P_r)\Psi_{k-1|k-1}(M_k P_r)^\top + Q_k$
        \State Compute $\Psi_{k|k}$ and $\alpha_{k|k}$ using~\eqref{eq:psiupdate} with SMW for $\Sigma_{k|k-1}^{-1}$
        \State Update $x_{k|k} \gets x_{k|k-1} + P_r \alpha_{k|k}$
        \State Store $x_{k|k}$ and $L_k$ such that $\Psi_{k|k}^{-1}=L_k L_k^\top$
        \EndFor
        \If{RTS smoothing is used}
        \For{$k=T-1$ down to $0$}
        \State Compute $C_k \gets M_{k} P_r L_k^{-\top}$
        \State Compute smoother gain $K_k^s$ and update $(\alpha_{k|T},\Psi_{k|T})$
        \State $x_{k|T} \gets x_{k|k} + P_r \alpha_{k|T}$
        \EndFor
        \EndIf
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
\section{Numerical experiments}
The neural network is implemented in PyTorch and trained on CSC Mahti HPC using a single compute node with 4 NVIDIA A100 40GB GPUs.

The Kalman filter is implemented in PyTorch to take advantage of GPU computing which leads to orders of magnitude reduction in wall clock time of filtering.
Using a single NVIDIA A100 40GB GPU, computing the prediction and update step takes 0.6 seconds in single precision, and 0.3 seconds in double precision with 2048 basis vectors and $256 \times 256$ dimensional state, and observation vectors without accounting for the time taken for observation forward model, and state model.

We assess empirically the approximation error of the reduced filter on coarsened COT data.
The test data is from the same region as in Figure~\ref{fig:examplecot}, but spatially coarsened to $97 \times 105$ grid to make the full reference filter computationally feasible.

Figure~\ref{fig:basisconvergence} shows the mean squared error between the reduced filter and full filter posterior means as a function of the reduced dimension $r$ in log-log scale averaged over 10 days of data with 26 assimilation steps.
The autoregressive model $M_k=I$ was used to ensure a consistent setup across different basis sizes.

The observed slope of $\approx -1.06$ is consistent with the one-step theoretical rate for a two-dimensional Matérn covariance with $\nu=1$.
Although the estimate is derived for a single analysis step under a common prior, the same observed rate averaged over time suggests that the dominant contribution of the approximation error is mainly governed by the covariance truncation.
